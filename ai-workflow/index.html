AI in Instructional Design — A Workflow Case Study

Yusi Xu · Senior Instructional Designer
peecatboo.github.io · yusixu@outlook.com

← Back to portfolio

Why this document exists

Most ID candidates can name AI tools. Fewer can speak to where AI fits in a regulated production environment — where source materials cannot leave the firm, where SMEs require verifiable accuracy, and where the cost of a single hallucinated clinical detail is unacceptable.

This case study walks through how AI is integrated into my production pipeline, anchored in Code Blue: Simulated Cardiac Arrest (live demo) — and how I keep that workflow inside the data-governance boundaries my employer and clients require.

The constraint that shapes everything

In healthcare ID, three categories of source content cannot be uploaded to third-party LLMs:

  1. Institutional policies
  2. Employer-proprietary content (existing courseware, internal frameworks)
  3. Client-provided content from healthcare organization customers

Only public materials — AHA 2020 ACLS guidelines, peer-reviewed literature, FDA-published documents — can be uploaded freely.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It rules out the "drop your PDFs into NotebookLM" workflow that dominates current ID-with-AI tutorials. It requires a different production pattern.

My pattern: outline first, then elaborate

The key move: AI never sees the protected source material directly. AI only sees an outline I have already written in my own words.

Protected source (e.g., institutional policy, client material)
         ↓
   I read it myself
         ↓
   I write a detailed outline in my own words
         ↓
   Outline → ChatGPT  AND  outline → Claude (same prompt, two outputs)
         ↓
   Compare drafts; cherry-pick or merge
         ↓
   Human edit pass
         ↓
   SME review

Two things this protects:

  • Proprietary or regulated content never leaves my machine in a form usable elsewhere.
  • The outline I feed AI is already filtered through clinical and instructional judgment — so AI's job becomes elaboration, not interpretation of sensitive source material.

Why I run both ChatGPT and Claude in parallel

For drafting work, I run the same outline through both ChatGPT and Claude, then pick the stronger draft or merge the two.

The tools fail in different ways. One run gives me one perspective; two runs give me a comparison I can use to catch generic phrasing, awkward transitions, or clinical claims that neither tool flagged independently. The second draft costs effectively nothing, and the pick-or-merge step is the fastest editorial pass I have.

This pattern only works if you have an outline strong enough that both tools are producing comparable drafts. If the outline is weak, both outputs are weak in the same way — and you have no signal.

Code Blue: what AI actually did

Phase What I did What AI did
Source synthesis Read AHA 2020 ACLS guidelines + institutional protocols; built detailed outline in my own words Nothing — protected sources stay local
Scenario design Designed the two-pathway structure, mapped 17 decision points to Bloom's L1–L4, wrote distractors Nothing — clinical judgment work
Transcript drafting Wrote outlines for each screen's narration Elaborated outlines into spoken-language transcripts (dual ChatGPT + Claude, picked stronger version per screen)
Voiceover and animation Specified voice characteristics, supplied hand-drawn character images, defined pacing ElevenLabs synthesized voice and generated lip-sync character animation from uploaded images and transcripts
Pronunciation QA Logged every flagged medical term from SME review Re-synthesized in ElevenLabs (phonetic spellings, pacing tags, re-records)

The ElevenLabs lip-sync workflow

For Code Blue, character animations are driven by ElevenLabs. I upload the hand-drawn 2D character image; ElevenLabs generates the voice from the transcript and produces lip-synced animation for that character. This replaces what used to require (a) voice talent and animator hired separately, or (b) skipping character animation altogether.

The pronunciation QA loop is non-negotiable

Medical pronunciation is the recurring failure mode. ElevenLabs reliably mispronounces specialized terminology — drug names, anatomical Latin, clinical acronyms. My standard loop:

  1. Initial synthesis from approved transcript
  2. SME plays through, flags every mispronunciation in a tracking doc
  3. I edit in ElevenLabs (phonetic spelling, pacing tags, or full re-record)
  4. SME re-checks
  5. Final SCORM build

In my experience, at least one specialized term needs phonetic correction in nearly every simulation we ship. Skipping this loop means a learner hears a key clinical term pronounced wrong in the middle of a high-stakes scenario — and the credibility of the entire course collapses. AI voice synthesis is fast; the QA cost of using it is what most production timelines underestimate.

Principles I hold

  1. AI never sees the protected source. If I cannot describe the content in my own outline, AI cannot draft from it.
  2. Two drafts beat one. Running the same task through ChatGPT and Claude in parallel produces better final output than picking one tool and iterating with it alone.
  3. AI is for first drafts, not final outputs — especially in clinical or other high-stakes contexts.
  4. The SME QA loop on AI-generated voiceover is non-negotiable. Mispronounced specialized terms break learner trust faster than almost anything else in a course.
  5. Don't hide AI usage from SMEs. Reviewers catch more — and review more carefully — when they know which sections were AI-drafted.

Tech stack

  • ChatGPT and Anthropic Claude (claude.ai) — dual-tool drafting and editorial review
  • ElevenLabs — voice synthesis and lip-sync character animation
  • Articulate Storyline 360 / Rise 360 — authoring and SCORM packaging (no AI in this layer)
